\section{Introduction}

Cybersystems today operate in a highly hostile environment, effectively described as
a state of war. Networks are under constant assault from a diverse array of malicious
actors---including cybercriminals, state-sponsored spies, disgruntled insiders, and
hacktivists---who exploit software flaws for financial gain, political agendas, or power.

To counter these threats, defenders have traditionally relied on a layered security
model. The \textbf{first line of defense} consists of Access Controls, which aim to
prevent unauthorized entry. However, these controls are frequently subverted by
vulnerabilities or misconfigurations. When access controls fail, defenders rely on
a \textbf{second line of defense}: Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS). While valuable,
traditional IDS are largely reactive; they depend on recognizing known ``signatures''
of malicious behavior, making them ineffective against novel (zero-day) attacks or
sophisticated variants.

This inadequacy of traditional mechanisms creates the critical need for a
\textbf{third line of defense}: \textbf{cyber deception}. As a form of ``Active
Defense,'' deception moves beyond merely spotting anomalies; it actively misleads
adversaries who have bypassed perimeter security. By deliberately injecting false
information---lying, cheating, and misleading---the system can waste an attacker's
resources and prevent them from achieving their goals, even after they have gained
access \cite{rowe2016introduction}.

Historically, the foundational implementation of this third line of defense was the
\textbf{honeypot} \cite{cohen2006use}. Defined by Lance Spitzner as a
\textbf{security resource whose value lies in being probed, attacked, or compromised},
these systems were utilized to track and analyze attacker methodologies in a contained
environment \cite{spitzner2002honeypots}. The technology evolved from simple,
low-interaction traps to versatile virtual frameworks like \textbf{Honeyd}
\cite{provos2004virtual}, which enabled the simulation of large-scale network
topologies to provide deep, actionable intelligence. However, despite these
advancements, initial solutions remained largely static and passively waited for
interaction, as noted in comprehensive surveys of that era \cite{nawrocki2016survey}.

As the field matured, the scope of deception expanded. Researchers began to formalize
the discipline, creating structured taxonomies to categorize techniques by objective
and deployment level \cite{han2018deception}. This formalization fueled new avenues
of research: scholars applied \textbf{game theory} to model the strategic interactions
between attackers and defenders, seeking to optimize the placement and timing of
deceptive artifacts \cite{carroll2011game,schlenker2018deceiving}. Concurrently,
other works explored the \textbf{cognitive} aspects of deception, investigating how
to manipulate the human attacker's decision-making processes rather than just their
automated tools \cite{ferguson2021examining}.

Today, the field is undergoing another fundamental shift, driven by an accelerating
``arms race'' powered by Artificial Intelligence (AI). Modern adversaries are no
longer just human operators; they are increasingly sophisticated, AI-driven agents
leveraging Large Language Models (LLMs) to automate complex attacks
\cite{deng2023pentestgpt,fang2024llm}. This evolution demands a corresponding leap
in defense, giving rise to \textbf{adaptive, AI-driven deception}, where systems
use Reinforcement Learning (RL) to orchestrate defense policies in real-time
\cite{hammad2024deep}.

This clear trajectory---from static traps to intelligent, predictive systems---presents
a complex research landscape. To map this evolution, this project conducts a Systematic
Literature Review (SLR) guided by the following research question:

\begin{quote}
    \textit{``What are the dominant paradigms, open challenges, and emerging research
        directions in cyber deception, particularly in the shift from static/reactive
        honeypots to adaptive, AI-driven and predictive deception systems?''}
\end{quote}

To answer this question, this review first outlines key definitions and concepts.
It then presents the results of the literature synthesis, structured around the
project's key target outcomes: the taxonomy of deception tactics, the implementation
of proactive strategies, the use of attacker profiling, and the emerging role of AI.
Finally, we discuss identified gaps and propose directions for future research.
