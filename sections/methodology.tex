\section{Key Definitions and Concepts}

Before analyzing the evolutionary trajectory of cyber deception, it is essential to
establish a precise vocabulary. The literature uses several overlapping terms to
describe defensive countermeasures. This section defines the core concepts that form
the basis of this review.

\begin{description}

      \item[Cyber Deception]
            Unlike traditional security measures that aim to deny access, cyber deception
            consists of deliberate actions taken by a defender to mislead an adversary.
            As defined by Han et al., it involves the manipulation of the attacker's
            perception, beliefs, or actions to cause them to fail or waste resources
            \cite{han2018deception}. It is a broad strategy that encompasses concealing
            the truth (dissimulation) and showing the false (simulation)
            \cite{rowe2016introduction,pawlick2019game}.


      \item[Honeypot]
            The honeypot is the foundational tool of cyber deception. According to Lance
            Spitzner's seminal definition, a honeypot is ``a security resource whose value
            lies in being probed, attacked, or compromised'' \cite{spitzner2002honeypots}.
            It is a decoy system that has no production value; therefore, any interaction
            with it is inherently suspicious. Honeypots are historically categorized as
            either \textit{low-interaction} (emulating services) or \textit{high-interaction}
            (providing real operating systems) \cite{provos2004virtual,nawrocki2016survey}.

      \item[Honey-Token]
            A deceptive data artifact (e.g., fake credentials, API keys, database entries)
            placed on a system to detect unauthorized access when exfiltrated or accessed.
            Any use of a honey-token triggers an alert, enabling early detection of
            compromises \cite{nawrocki2016survey}.


      \item[Active Defense]
            This term refers to a proactive cybersecurity doctrine that contrasts with
            passive defense. Active defense involves real-time engagement with the adversary.
            It is the operational framework within which modern deception is often deployed.
            The goal is to raise the cost of the attack and increase the risk of detection
            for the adversary \cite{jajodia2011moving}.


      \item[Moving Target Defense (MTD)]
            Often integrated with deception, MTD is a strategy that aims to create
            ``asymmetric uncertainty'' for the attacker. It involves dynamically shifting
            the system's attack surface to invalidate the intelligence collected by an
            attacker during their reconnaissance phase \cite{jajodia2011moving}.


      \item[Game Theory in Security]
            A mathematical framework used extensively in modern deception research to
            model the strategic interaction between a rational attacker and a defender.
            In this context, deception is viewed as a game where the defender attempts
            to optimize their payoff by predicting the attacker's moves based on their
            incentives and available information.
            \cite{carroll2011game,zhu2015game,pawlick2019game}. In particular,
            \textbf{Signaling Games}---where one player transmits signals that influence
            another player's beliefs---have become central to modeling deceptive
            interactions \cite{pawlick2019game}.



      \item[Adaptive Deception]
            An emerging paradigm where the deception strategy is not static but evolves
            in response to the attacker's behavior. In recent literature, this is often
            achieved through Artificial Intelligence techniques, such as Reinforcement
            Learning (RL), which allow the system to learn optimal defense policies through
            trial-and-error interactions with the threat \cite{hammad2024deep}.

\end{description}
